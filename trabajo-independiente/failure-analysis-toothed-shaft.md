*Practice exercise (ungraded) proposed in the Failure Analysis and Prevention course, Universidad del Norte (2026). The origin and service history of the part are unknown.*

## Summary

A fractured toothed-end shaft of unknown origin was examined by visual inspection, stereomicroscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The fracture shows no necking or macroscopic plastic deformation, a bright faceted surface, three fracture planes inclined at approximately 45° to the shaft axis, and chevron marks pointing to one origin in each plane. SEM fractography revealed a **mixed fracture mode**: intergranular separation along grain boundaries, together with transgranular regions showing river marks. Corrosion products were found on the fracture surface, and a small globular feature, interpreted as an inclusion, was observed on one grain facet; its composition was not identified.

The evidence supports a **brittle fracture under a predominantly torsional load with multiple crack origins**. The available evidence is not sufficient to confirm fatigue, to determine when the corrosion developed, or to identify the cause of the intergranular separation.

## 1. Component

The part is a shaft with an external toothed section. The fracture is located just before the start of the toothed section. The measured addendum (outside) diameter of the toothed section is 29.537 mm (micrometer, resolution 0.001 mm). Three threaded holes on the opposite end are probably the attachment through which the input torque was transmitted. Based on its geometry, the part was likely a power-transmission shaft from a reduction or clutch system.

## 4. SEM fractography

At low magnification, corrosion products appear as bright, irregular deposits on the fracture surface ({numref}`fig-shaft-sem-120`). At higher magnification the surface shows polyhedral grain facets with smooth faces and sharp edges: **intergranular separation** ({numref}`fig-shaft-sem-1000`, {numref}`fig-shaft-sem-5000`).

The fracture is not fully intergranular. Some facets show **river marks**, the branching steps typical of transgranular cleavage, in which the crack crosses the grain instead of following its boundary ({numref}`fig-shaft-sem-1200`, {numref}`fig-shaft-sem-3000`). The fracture mode is therefore **mixed intergranular–transgranular**.

A dark, roughly spherical feature about 1 µm in diameter is visible on one transgranular facet ({numref}`fig-shaft-sem-3000`). It was interpreted as a **globular inclusion**, but its composition was not determined.

## 5. Hypotheses evaluated

**H1. Initial misalignment of the gear mesh.** Misalignment would concentrate the contact load on part of the tooth face and add bending to the shaft, which could initiate cracks and drive high-cycle fatigue. *Supporting evidence:* the wear and plastic deformation are localized on two teeth instead of being distributed. *Against or missing:* no beach marks were seen, and no fatigue striations were identified in the SEM images, so fatigue was not confirmed. The tooth damage could also have occurred during or after the fracture.

**H2. Environmental embrittlement of the material.** Corrosive agents in the environment could have embrittled the material in the fracture zone. *Supporting evidence:* corrosion products on the fracture surface and intergranular separation. *Against or missing:* the fracture is not fully intergranular, the corrosion could have formed after the fracture during storage, and no hardness, chemical or metallographic data are available to identify a specific embrittlement mechanism.

## 6. Conclusions

1. **Brittle fracture.** There is no necking or macroscopic plastic deformation, the surface is bright and faceted, and chevron marks are present.
2. **Predominantly torsional loading.** In a material that behaves in a brittle manner, the maximum principal tensile stress under pure torsion acts at 45° to the axis, which produces helical fracture planes like the ones observed. A ductile shaft overloaded in torsion would instead fail on a flat transverse plane by shear.
3. **Multiple origins.** Each of the three fracture planes has its own origin near the outer surface.
4. **Mixed micromechanism.** The SEM shows both intergranular separation and transgranular cleavage, with a globular inclusion of unknown composition.

**Limitations.** The material, heat treatment, service conditions and storage history are unknown. The evidence is insufficient to confirm fatigue, to date the corrosion, or to establish why the grain boundaries failed.

## 7. Discussion: points not resolved by the evidence

*This section was added after the practice exercise. It separates what the evidence shows from possible explanations that would still need to be tested.*

**Why is the fracture mixed?** Intergranular fracture in steel indicates that the grain boundaries were weaker than the grains at the moment of fracture. The observations do not identify the cause, but several common explanations would produce a mixed fracture:

- **Case-hardened shaft.** Toothed shafts are often carburized or induction hardened. A hard, high-carbon case can fracture intergranularly while the softer core fractures transgranularly. In that case, the mixed mode would reflect the case–core microstructure rather than degradation.
- **Hydrogen embrittlement.** Hydrogen-assisted cracking is often intergranular near the origin, where the crack grows slowly, and becomes transgranular as the crack accelerates.
- **Grain boundary embrittlement from heat treatment.** Examples are temper embrittlement or overheating during austenitizing.
- **Intergranular stress corrosion cracking.** This would require the corrosion to be present before the fracture, not only after it.

These alternatives can be distinguished with tests the laboratory can perform. A **microhardness traverse** on a longitudinal section would show whether a hardened case exists and how deep it is. **Mapping the intergranular regions** relative to the surface and to the origins would show whether they are confined to the case or to the origin zones. A **metallographic section** etched to reveal the prior austenite grains would show the case–core structure and any grain boundary features.

**Is the corrosion older than the fracture?** Corrosion that formed before the fracture tends to be thicker and concentrated near the origins, sometimes penetrating along the grain boundaries. Corrosion that formed after the fracture tends to cover all exposed surfaces uniformly, including the final fracture zone. The darker color of plane N1 is compatible with a crack that was open longer, but color alone does not date the corrosion. A metallographic section through an origin would show whether corrosion products penetrate below the fracture surface.

**What is the globular inclusion?** At about 1 µm, the feature is small compared with the grain facets, which are roughly 15–25 µm across. The chevron marks also point to origins at the outer surface, not at this feature. It is therefore unlikely to be the crack origin and is probably an incidental feature of the steel. In secondary-electron images an inclusion and a small void can look alike. An **EDS point analysis** would show whether it is a sulfide (Mn, S), an oxide (Al, Si, O) or a void.

**Why three origins?** Several origins around the circumference usually indicate either a high nominal stress, such as an overload or impact torque, or a stress concentration acting around the whole perimeter. The fracture is located just before the toothed section, where the change of section and the end of the teeth concentrate stress. The fillet radius at that transition was not measured and should be included in further work.

**Recommended further work:** hardness and microhardness traverse; metallographic section through an origin; EDS on the inclusion and on the corrosion products; chemical composition by optical emission spectroscopy (OES) to identify the steel; measurement of the fillet radius at the transition to the toothed section.
